## Title
**Emergency Contact**
Alternatives: *Someone From Work* · *Naa Manishi* (My Person)

## Logline
After a rain-slick scooty accident, a fiercely independent Hyderabad designer wakes up to find her no-label situationship at her bedside — because her ex quietly listed him as her emergency contact months ago, having realised who she called when life went wrong. Caught between the man who shows up but won't name it and the man who named it but never showed up, she has to decide whether love means settling for half.

## Genre and tone
Romantic drama, restrained and bittersweet. Small rooms, late nights, phone screens, unsaid things. Humour is dry and quiet; emotion lives in pauses, glances and what characters almost say. No villains, no slaps, no melodrama — the biggest scenes are a pen hovering over a form and a phone call taken on speaker.

## Theme
Love is two things — showing up and saying it out loud — and you are allowed to ask for both. Being "independent" can also be a way of never asking.

## Central dramatic question
Will Meera keep accepting half a love — presence without a name, or a name without presence — or will she finally ask, out loud, for the whole thing?

## Protagonist(s) and what they want vs need
- **Meera Rao (28)**, UX designer, lives alone in Gachibowli, handles everything herself, protects her widowed mother in Vijayawada from every worry. *Wants:* to need nobody, and to keep things with Karthik "easy". *Needs:* to admit that she already chose someone long ago — and to ask to be chosen back, in words.
- **Karthik (30)**, sound engineer, the calmest person in any crisis. *Wants:* to keep Meera without the label he believes breaks things. *Needs:* to understand that refusing to name something is its own way of leaving.
- **Arjun Varma (31)**, consultant, Meera's ex who proposed. *Wants:* a second chance and the future he planned. *Needs:* to accept the truth he already wrote down on a hospital form seven months ago.

## Antagonistic force
Not a person — fear in two shapes. Karthik's fear that naming love invites its loss (he watched his father leave his mother after twenty years). Arjun's habit of loving through plans instead of presence. And Meera's own pride, which would rather be alone than ask.

## The hook
"Nurse, he's not my boyfriend." / "Okay. Do you want him to leave?" / A long silence. Every viewer has been in that silence. Then he stays all night — and in the morning introduces her to his mother as "someone from work". Instant ache, instant question: *what are they?*

## Shape of the story (beginning, midpoint, ending, in a paragraph each)
**Beginning (Eps 1-3).** Meera wakes in Aarogya Hospital with a fractured wrist and Karthik asleep in the chair. He stays the night, then calls her "someone from work" to his mother. Meera learns he was called because he is her listed emergency contact — a form she never filled. The handwriting is Arjun's, from the night seven months ago when he admitted her with dengue and heard her, half-conscious, calling for Karthik.

**Midpoint (Eps 4-6).** Arjun admits what he saw and why he let her go — and asks for another chance, promising he has changed. Meera finally asks Karthik why she is "someone from work"; he walks out rather than answer. She says yes to a date with Arjun, who puts his phone away and plans their wedding without noticing she is in pain. Then Karthik calls her, frightened: his mother has been admitted in Warangal. For the first time, he is the one calling her when life goes wrong.

**Ending (Eps 7-10).** In Warangal, Karthik's mother tells Meera why her son is afraid of names. Asked to add Meera to his mother's emergency form, Karthik freezes. Meera steps back from both men: she turns down Arjun's family lunch and his ring, and stops being Karthik's unnamed emergency. At her cast-removal appointment the nurse asks her to update her emergency contact — the same long silence — and Karthik arrives, answers his mother's call on speaker, and names her: "naa manishi". Meera hands him the pen. Arjun leaves for Pune.

## The ending and its emotional payoff
The opening silence returns, but this time Meera breaks it on her own terms: she doesn't run into Karthik's arms, she hands him the form and makes him write it himself — relationship column included. Asked again, "So... boyfriend?", she smiles: "Inka decide cheyyaledu." The payoff is a woman who stopped settling, a man who said it out loud in front of witnesses, and an ex who was right about everything except his own place in the story — the bittersweet ache that he saw her clearly too late.

## Why it works for urban Telugu viewers 20-35 (Hyderabad, Bengaluru, Vijayawada), mobile-first on this format
"Situationship" is the defining relationship of this audience — modern, unlabelled, anxious. Every viewer knows a Karthik and an Arjun, and the code-mixed Hyderabad Telugu sounds like their own group chats. The premise is built on phone-screen moments (calls, forms, texts on speaker) that read perfectly in vertical framing. Each two-minute episode turns on a single small, recognisable humiliation or tenderness, which drives comments ("Team Karthik / Team Arjun") and binge-swiping. Family pressure (mothers, lunches, "someone from work") grounds it in Telugu reality without melodrama.

## Risks to watch (cultural, logical, pacing)
- **Logic:** hospitals call the contact on file, so the form must plausibly be from the same hospital network — set it up clearly (dengue admission, same hospital, seven months ago).
- **Character:** Karthik must never look careless — his avoidance needs a visible cause (his mother's story) before the payoff, or the ending is unearned.
- **Arjun must not be a villain:** give him real insight and a dignified exit, so the choice isn't easy.
- **Pacing:** restraint can read as slow on mobile — every episode still needs a hook in the first seconds and a concrete turn.
- **Cultural:** the family lunch and mothers should feel warm and real, not cartoonish pressure; avoid shaming Meera for living alone or for the situationship.
- **Language:** Telugu must stay spoken and natural (vyavaharika, Hyderabad register) with English where people really use it — no bookish lines.
